# Double-entry bookkeeping, debits and credits

## The core rule
Every transaction touches at least two accounts. The total of the debits always equals the total of the credits, so the books balance by construction. The accounting equation always holds: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Income increases equity and expenses decrease it, which is why profit flows into Retained Earnings at year end.

## Single entry vs double entry
Single entry is a running log of money in and money out, like a checkbook register. It is simple but has no built-in check. Double entry records each transaction as a debit in one account and a credit in another, so total debits equal total credits and errors show up as an out-of-balance trial balance. QuickBooks Online is double entry; it writes both sides for you.

## Which side increases which account
- Assets: a debit increases, a credit decreases (cash, receivables, equipment).
- Expenses: a debit increases, a credit decreases.
- Owner draws and distributions: a debit increases.
- Liabilities: a credit increases, a debit decreases (credit cards, loans, payables).
- Equity: a credit increases, a debit decreases.
- Income: a credit increases, a debit decreases.
Memory aid: DEALER. Dividends/draws, Expenses, Assets increase with debits; Liabilities, Equity, Revenue increase with credits.

## Everyday examples
- Pay rent by check: debit Rent Expense, credit Checking.
- Buy supplies on a credit card: debit Supplies, credit the credit card liability.
- Pay the credit card bill: debit the credit card liability, credit Checking. This is a transfer, not an expense; the expense was recorded when the purchase was made.
- Invoice a customer: debit Accounts Receivable, credit Income.
- Receive the customer's payment: debit Checking (or Undeposited Funds), credit Accounts Receivable.
- Enter a vendor bill: debit the expense, credit Accounts Payable. Pay the bill: debit Accounts Payable, credit Checking.

## Why this matters for cleanup
A balance on the "wrong" side (a negative bank, a negative liability, a debit balance in an income account) usually means one side of an entry was posted to the wrong account, or a transaction was entered twice, or a payment was recorded without the bill or invoice it pays.
